\begin{abstract}
Accurate estimation of tree biomass and the carbon contained within it is fundamental for credible carbon accounting and verification for tree planting and offsetting schemes (including the UK's tree planting target and the associated Woodland Carbon Code), and broader climate mitigation and forest management strategies. Reliable estimates are also essential for assessing forests' contribution to national carbon budgets and nature-based climate solutions.

Destructive sampling data is scarce, and existing allometric equations differ in their mathematical form, species and biome specificity, and assumptions about measurement protocols and biomass-to-carbon conversion factors, which can lead to divergent estimates of carbon stocks even for similar input variables. These inconsistencies, compounded by uncertainty in the measurement of DBH, height, and wood density, limit confidence in reported carbon stocks.

We present TreeCarbon, an open-source R package for calculating and comparing tree- and stand-level biomass and carbon estimates using multiple established methods (Woodland Carbon Code 2021, Bunce 1968, the allodb R package and BIOMASS R package), within a UK and temperate context. This package integrates uncertainty propagation, batch processing, and summary functions for carbon at the individual level or per hectare. A companion interactive Shiny app enables users to explore and visualise differences among methods without the coding overhead.

The package brings together multiple tree allometries and other routines, allowing a direct comparison of these methods.

TreeCarbon v1.0 is available on GitHub github.com/izaopenshaw/TreeCarbon. By standardising access to multiple allometric models and quantifying uncertainty, TreeCarbon supports more transparent, reproducible, and comparable tree carbon assessments.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Global increases in greenhouse gas concentrations and the ongoing loss of forest cover have increased the pressure to manage and protect terrestrial carbon stocks. Forest ecosystems play a crucial role in temperature regulation and atmospheric balance by sequestering CO$_2$ and offering a wide range of ecosystem services to mitigate climate change. Yet more than half of the planet's woody forests have been lost, with the United Kingdom having lost approximately 84\% of its initial forest cover \citep{Watts2006}.

Habitat protection, halting deforestation, and expanding tree planting can help balance atmospheric CO$_2$ by preserving carbon sinks, reducing emissions from tree loss, and increasing carbon sequestration through photosynthesis. Trees absorb CO$_2$ and store it in their biomass and in turn the soil while also delivering broader ecosystem services such as improving air-quality, hydrological and climate-regulation benefits, soil stabilisation and biodiversity support.

Quantifying forest biomass, and therefore the carbon it contains, underpins carbon accounting, financing and offsetting initiatives. These estimates form the basis for nature-based carbon markets and management strategies, transparent and comparable carbon estimation is essential for credible carbon markets. In the UK, the WCC is the primary verification standard. Reliable biomass and carbon estimation is also crucial for compliance with international and national frameworks. The IPCC Land Use, Land-Use Change and Forestry guidelines, the Verified Carbon Standard (Verra), and the WCC all require consistent, traceable methods for carbon reporting and credit certification.

Despite the central role of these estimates in carbon markets and forest policy, substantial uncertainty persists in estimating both the magnitude and spatial distribution of forest biomass. This uncertainty arises from limited sampling, measurement variability, and model assumptions, constraining our ability to determine how much carbon is absorbed in woodlands. However, substantial inconsistencies exist between allometric equations and carbon estimation protocols. Biomass estimates for the same stand can vary widely depending on the chosen equation \citep{Chave2014}. These discrepancies arise from differences in model form, calibration datasets, and geographic or taxonomic scope. Many equations are derived from forest plantations or tropical species and may not perform reliably in temperate systems or mature trees. Divergent assumptions about wood density, measurement protocols, and biomass-to-carbon conversion factors further compound these inconsistencies, limiting the comparability of results across studies and projects. \citet{Calders2022} showed that in an English woodland, nearly 50\% of above-ground biomass was concentrated in just 7\% of the largest trees---a component largely under-represented in current UK carbon accounts.

Direct measurement of tree biomass requires destructive sampling, which is of course contradictory of conservation goals as well as being time consuming and costly. To overcome this limitation, allometric equations have been developed to estimate woody volume indirectly from measurable attributes such as diameter at breast height (DBH), height, and wood density. These equations are typically derived from small destructive datasets, often measured in young or managed stands rather than mature natural forests. In the UK, commonly applied methods include those developed by \citet{Bunce1968} and the WCC \citep{ForestryCommission2022}. Trees are biodiverse generally and within species, and each tree may interact differently with its local environment even for the same species and same local conditions. This leads to trees displaying widely variable growth patterns. Therefore, no single allometric equation can provide an exact estimate of a tree's biomass. The main species-level biomass variation factors include location, climate, water availability, soil type, competition, genetics, and age. As trees mature, these deviations from model assumptions substantially increase, with the propagation of their deviations confounding and multiple more interactions at play.

To reduce variation and bias, multi-model or weighted approaches have been proposed. For example, the allodb R package \citep{Gonzalez-Akre2022} combines multiple allometric models across boreal and temperate regions using sample-size, climate, and taxonomic weighting. In addition to biological variability, there are inherent measurement uncertainties in DBH, height, and wood density, and conversion uncertainties when translating biomass to carbon due to differences in wood chemistry. The propagation of these uncertainties through allometric equations can have large effects on resultant biomass and carbon values; the BIOMASS R package \citep{Rejou-Mechain2017} implements pan-tropical allometries and propagates uncertainty through Monte Carlo simulations, enabling quantification of model precision and the combined effects of measurement error and model variability on biomass estimates.

Despite widespread recognition of these issues, few tools allow users to apply multiple allometric models within a unified, transparent, and uncertainty-aware framework. Here, `uncertainty' refers to the combined effects of measurement error, model variability, and conversion factors on estimated biomass and carbon, which encompasses both model precision and accuracy. Most available software implements only one method or omits uncertainty propagation altogether, forcing users to rely on ad hoc calculations that hinder reproducibility. Because small variations in input measurements (such as DBH, height, or wood density) can translate into large variations in estimated biomass, a clear quantification of uncertainty is essential.

Here we present TreeCarbon, an open-source R package and companion Shiny application that integrates several widely used biomass and carbon estimation methods (Bunce 1968; WCC 2021; allodb; BIOMASS). The package propagates uncertainty through allometric calculations, supports batch processing, and provides summary statistics of carbon per hectare. By consolidating multiple models into a single, reproducible workflow, TreeCarbon enables comparison of estimates across methods, evaluation of different model assumptions, and reporting of confidence in the forest carbon estimates. In the UK context, TreeCarbon facilitates direct comparison of the WCC with alternative models, highlighting methodological differences and their implications for carbon estimates.

\section{Materials and methods}

All code was developed in R 4.3.1 \citep{RCore2023}. TreeCarbon's declared \texttt{Imports} are used as follows. \texttt{allodb} \citep{Gonzalez-Akre2022} and \texttt{BIOMASS} \citep{Rejou-Mechain2017} provide the extratropical meta-allometry and pan-tropical biomass pipelines (species-parameter estimation, \texttt{get\_biomass} / \texttt{computeAGB}, wood-density look-up, optional height--diameter models, and Monte Carlo AGB when uncertainty is requested) that TreeCarbon wraps and runs beside WCC and Bunce routines. Functions from \texttt{utils} load package datasets (\texttt{data()}), take neighbouring rows when interpolating height--volume relationships, and may show text progress bars on long runs. The \texttt{stats} namespace supplies random variates, quantiles, moments, and related summaries used in Monte Carlo and analytical uncertainty propagation, and for ordering factors in diagnostic plots. Plotting and string handling use \texttt{ggplot2} and \texttt{stringr} \citep{Wickham2019tidyverse}: \texttt{stringr} trims and tokenises taxonomic and vernacular names for species matching, and \texttt{ggplot2} produces method-comparison, sensitivity, and uncertainty figures; \texttt{scales} adds colour palettes (e.g.\ perceptually uniform scales when several allometries are overlaid). \texttt{remotes} is included so users can install GitHub-hosted dependencies---notably \texttt{allodb}---when setting up the environment.

Suggested (\texttt{Suggests}) software extends use outside the core library. \texttt{shiny} \citep{Chang2024shiny}, \texttt{shinydashboard}, \texttt{plotly}, \texttt{DT}, and \texttt{shinyBS} implement the interactive applications (layouts, linked graphics, sortable tables, and modal help). \texttt{knitr} and \texttt{rmarkdown} build vignettes and reproducible reports \citep{Xie2015knitr,Xie2018rmarkdown}. \texttt{testthat} runs the package unit tests. \texttt{MASS} is used when correlated input uncertainties are propagated via multivariate normal simulation. \texttt{writexl} enables Excel export from the Shiny tools. The package metadata additionally lists \texttt{tidyr} and \texttt{httr2} as optional suggestions for auxiliary workflows; they are not invoked by the exported estimation routines themselves.

\subsection{Allometric equations and inputs}

TreeCarbon builds on existing open-source resources, integrating methodologies from allodb \citep{Gonzalez-Akre2022} and BIOMASS \citep{Rejou-Mechain2017} within a unified structure alongside the Forestry Commission's WCC and Bunce \citep{Bunce1968} equations. The TreeCarbon package standardises input requirements (DBH, height, species, location), propagates uncertainty across all methods, and enables batch and comparative analyses. This enables assessment of how methodological choice affects carbon outcomes. While allodb and BIOMASS provide important regional or biome-specific models, TreeCarbon focuses on comparability and uncertainty propagation within the UK and temperate context, where the WCC remains the official standard.

Allometries for tree biomass are equations that estimate the biomass using various inputs associated with that tree. Allometric equations are largely determined at the species level using measurement of Diameter at Breast Height (DBH; occasionally diameter at trunk height), and often combined with tree height (not always needed for some volume allometries). Other variables often used include wood density and, for carbon, a conversion factor of biomass to carbon (both of which are often location or biome specific). Occasionally crown volumes are used \citep{Sillett2019}, but not usually in the UK.

\subsubsection{Geometric forms}

The simplest estimate of tree biomass uses straightforward geometry, where the tree's volume is estimated as a cylinder, cone or a truncated cone. These can work well for simple tree forms (e.g.\ pines) and in ideal growing conditions. The biomass is:
\begin{equation}
  \text{AGB} = V \times w_d
\end{equation}
where AGB is above-ground biomass (kg), $V$ is volume (m$^3$), and $w_d$ is the wood density for the species (kg/m$^3$).

This equation could be used to form a basis for error checking for outlier results from more complex allometries or other issues.

\subsubsection{Bunce}

Many simple tree biomass allometries follow the form given by \citet{Bunce1968} for a broadleaf tree:
\begin{equation}
  \ln(\text{AGB}) = a + b \ln(\pi \times \text{dbh})
\end{equation}
where dbh is diameter at breast height (m). Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
  \text{AGB} = \exp\bigl(a + b \ln(\pi \times \text{dbh})\bigr)
\end{equation}
TreeCarbon implements this form with species-specific coefficients $(a, b)$ from Bunce (1968). This formula has been built upon in many different allometric models following its publication in 1968.

\subsubsection{UK Woodland Carbon Code}

The WCC was set up to give quality assurance standards for UK woodland carbon projects. Here we implement the single-tree measures as assessed in version 2.0 of the assessment protocol \citep{Jenkins2018}; other measures use mean height and DBH by stand. The single tree measures used are broadly grouped by broadleaf and conifer, and then species-specific look-ups as constants for the allometric equations. Seven broadleaf species and twelve conifers are specifically identified, but they extend to other species being allocated to these standard species as proxies.

Other allometries like the WCC in the UK are based on forestry measures. In particular, the WCC \citep{ForestryCommission2022} was developed using a tree tariff number, which is defined and used in forestry for the useful timber within a tree \citep{Matthews2006}. The WCC combines tariff-based stem volume with crown biomass. Per the WCC protocol \citep{Jenkins2018}, the tariff number for each tree is calculated from Equations~\ref{eq:tariff-broadleaf}--\ref{eq:tariff-conifer} below and rounded to the nearest whole number; when multiple trees are averaged (e.g.\ for stand-level methods), the mean tariff is rounded down. This tariff number is then used to calculate the stem volume of the tree (using basal area as well). Stem biomass is calculated from this volume multiplied by the nominal specific gravity (nsg) \citep{Lavers1983}. Note that 30 species are represented for nsg, with other species allocated to a known nsg species.

\textbf{Tariff number} (rounded to nearest whole for each tree):
\begin{align}
  T_{\text{broadleaf}} &= a_1 + (a_2 \times h) + (a_3 \times \text{dbh}) + (a_4 \times \text{dbh} \times h) \label{eq:tariff-broadleaf} \\
  T_{\text{conifer}} &= a_1 + (a_2 \times h) + (a_3 \times \text{dbh}) \label{eq:tariff-conifer}
\end{align}
where $h$ is timber height (broadleaf) or total height (conifer) in m, dbh is in cm, and $a_1$--$a_4$ are species-specific constants \citep{Jenkins2018}.

\textbf{Volume} (Equation 5, \citealt{Jenkins2018}):
\begin{equation}
  V = b_1 + (b_2 \times \text{ba})
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
  b_1 &= (0.0360541 \times T) - (b_2 \times 0.118288) \nonumber \\
  b_2 &= 0.315049301 \times (T - 0.138763302) \nonumber \\
  \text{ba} &= \frac{\pi \times \text{dbh}^2}{40000}
\end{align}
with dbh in cm.

\textbf{Stem biomass:}
\begin{equation}
  \text{Stem biomass} = V \times \text{nsg}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Crown biomass:}
\begin{align}
  \text{Crown biomass (dbh} < 50\,\text{cm)} &= b \times \text{dbh}^p \\
  \text{Crown biomass (dbh} \geq 50\,\text{cm)} &= c + d \times \text{dbh}
\end{align}

\subsubsection{allodb}

The allodb package \citep{Gonzalez-Akre2022} provides biomass estimation for extratropical forests using a database of 570 parsed allometric equations from 701 woody species at 24 Forest Global Earth Observatory (ForestGEO) forest dynamic plots \citep{Anderson-Teixeira2015, Davies2021}. Rather than selecting a single best model, allodb uses a weighting function approach that combines multiple published allometric equations. For each species and location, the package calibrates new equation parameters $(a, b, \sigma)$ for the power-law form
\begin{equation}
  \text{AGB} = a \times \text{dbh}^b
\end{equation}
by resampling and weighting the original equations based on three criteria: (1) sample size, where larger samples receive higher weights to reflect greater statistical reliability; (2) taxonomic similarity, where equations for the same or closely related species are prioritised; and (3) climatic similarity, where equations from climatically similar locations receive higher weights. The calibration process involves resampling the original equation database to generate species- and location-specific parameters, effectively creating a meta-model that leverages the collective information from multiple published studies. The package requires genus, species, DBH, and geographic coordinates, and automatically selects appropriate models based on taxonomic and environmental matching. For species not in the database, allodb uses taxonomic proximity (genus or family-level) to assign models. This approach incorporates models calibrated across temperate and boreal regions.

\subsubsection{BIOMASS}

BIOMASS \citep{Rejou-Mechain2017} is an R package designed to estimate above-ground biomass (AGB) from forest inventory data in tropical and subtropical forests. It implements the pan-tropical allometric models developed by \citet{Chave2014}, accounts for environmental and taxonomic variability, and propagates uncertainty through Monte Carlo simulations. The package allows users to integrate wood density data, height--diameter relationships, and carbon conversion factors to generate consistent and reproducible AGB and carbon stock estimates. Whilst BIOMASS is based on pan-tropical species and regions, it can be applied to the UK. This is particularly useful for comparisons and especially for non-native or more horticultural tree species.

\subsection{Variables and their uncertainty}

\subsubsection{Species and common names}

TreeCarbon includes a lookup table (\texttt{lookup\_df}) that maps common names to scientific names and species codes used by each method. For WCC, species are matched to 7 broadleaf and 12 conifer proxy species. For allodb and BIOMASS, scientific names are matched against their respective databases. When a species is not found: WCC falls back to generic broadleaf/conifer coefficients; allodb uses taxonomic proximity (genus or family-level matching); BIOMASS uses regional wood density averages and generic allometries; Bunce falls back to generic broadleaf coefficients.

\subsubsection{DBH}

Diameter at breast height (DBH) is a standard metric used to assess a tree's size, growth and biomass. It is usually measured at a height of 1.3--1.4\,m above the ground, avoiding any swelling from the stump. Measurements are often made using two techniques: callipers or measuring its circumference using a tape (girth). DBH can also be measured using other survey methods including offset distance and angle, wedge prism relascope, or from photogrammetry or LiDAR scans. Each of these methods will bring different inaccuracies. Indeed, it is difficult to know which is giving the true DBH for trees, which will rarely conform to a perfect circle in cross section. \citet{Sun2019} compared callipers to tape measurement and showed errors ranging from 4.62\% to 2.65\%, with a mean error of 3.14\%, assuming the callipers are correct. All functions accept DBH in centimetres (cm), which is standard for UK forestry. Internal conversions to metres are performed where required by equations. The default relative error for DBH is 5\% ($r_{\text{dbh}} = 0.05$), based on Sun et al.\ (2019).

\subsubsection{Tree height}

Tree height is often used in combination with DBH to give biomass, but also when viewing from above (e.g.\ LiDAR and remote sensing) it may be the only variable available. Ground-based measurement methods are numerous, but most are based on trigonometry. The uncertainty associated with tree height estimates is due to the inherent measurement problems, which increase with tree height. \citet{Chave2004} suggest a standard error of ca.\ 10\% of the estimated height for ground measurements. We suggest 10\% error is appropriate for ground measurements, but the user can define their own uncertainty.

\subsubsection{Wood density}

Wood density (nominal specific gravity, NSG) is obtained from WCC lookup tables (30 species) or from the BIOMASS package database when using allodb/BIOMASS methods. The BIOMASS package provides species-specific wood density with uncertainty estimates derived from global databases \citep{Zanne2009}. Default uncertainty for NSG in WCC is $\sigma = 0.094$\,g/cm$^3$ \citep{Lavers1983}, representing variation in UK timber properties.

\subsubsection{Biomass to carbon}

Converting biomass to carbon (referred to as carbon fraction) for trees changes depending on the tissue chemistry (predominantly wood, oils and organic volatiles) of that species. The greatest difference globally is between Gymnosperms and Angiosperms, which largely for UK trees translates to conifers vs.\ broadleaf species. We implement multiple methods and fractions with confidence intervals (Matthews, IPCC, Thomas; see package documentation). For the UK we suggest using the default of Thomas et al.\ (2012) for temperate biomes, which gives a carbon fraction for broadleaves of 50.80\% and conifers of 48.80\%, with a 95\% confidence interval of 0.6\%.

\subsection{Propagating errors}

TreeCarbon propagates error for the carbon calculation methods through multiple approaches depending on the allometric method and equations used. The primary methods are analytical error propagation using first-order Taylor expansion and Monte Carlo simulation for non-linear relationships.

\subsubsection{Analytical error propagation}

For functions that can be approximated using linear equations, TreeCarbon uses first-order Taylor expansion following \citet{Chave2004} and \citet{Taylor1997}. For a function $f(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n)$ with uncertainties $\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \ldots, \sigma_n$, the combined uncertainty is:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_f = \sqrt{\sum_i \left( \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i} \sigma_i \right)^2}
\end{equation}

For multiplicative relationships (e.g.\ biomass $= V \times \rho$), relative errors are combined in quadrature:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_f = |f| \sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_a}{a}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\sigma_b}{b}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\sigma_c}{c}\right)^2}
\end{equation}
where $f = a \times b \times c$ and $\sigma_a$, $\sigma_b$, $\sigma_c$ are the uncertainties in each variable.

\subsubsection{Method-specific error propagation}

\textbf{WCC:} The WCC method propagates uncertainty through each step: (1) tariff number; (2) volume estimation; (3) stem biomass; (4) crown biomass; (5) carbon conversion. The total WCC uncertainty is:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_{\text{AGC}} = \sqrt{\sigma_{\text{stem}}^2 + \sigma_{\text{crown}}^2 + \sigma_{\text{CF}}^2}
\end{equation}

\textbf{BIOMASS:} Uses Monte Carlo simulation (default $n = 1000$ iterations) for uncertainty propagation.

\textbf{allodb:} TreeCarbon propagates parameter uncertainty analytically for the power-law $\text{AGB} = a \times \text{dbh}^b$:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_{\text{AGB}} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\partial\text{AGB}}{\partial a} \sigma_a\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial\text{AGB}}{\partial b} \sigma_b\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial\text{AGB}}{\partial\text{dbh}} \sigma_{\text{dbh}}\right)^2}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Bunce:} For $\text{AGB} = \exp(a + b \ln(\pi \times \text{dbh}))$, uncertainty propagates through the logarithmic term:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_{\text{AGB}} = \text{AGB} \sqrt{\sigma_a^2 + \left(\frac{b \sigma_{\text{dbh}}}{\text{dbh}}\right)^2 + \bigl(\ln(\pi \times \text{dbh}) \sigma_b\bigr)^2}
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_a$ and $\sigma_b$ are the standard errors of the regression coefficients from Bunce (1968), and $\sigma_{\text{dbh}}$ is the DBH measurement uncertainty.

\subsubsection{Total error propagation (summing multiple trees)}

When calculating total carbon or biomass across multiple trees, individual tree errors are combined in quadrature (assuming independence):
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_{\text{total}} = \sqrt{\sum_i \sigma_i^2}
\end{equation}
The relative error (coefficient of variation) for the total is:
\begin{equation}
  \text{CV}_{\text{total}} = \frac{\sigma_{\text{total}}}{\text{total}} = \frac{\sqrt{\sum_i \sigma_i^2}}{\sum_i x_i}
\end{equation}
where $x_i$ is the carbon/biomass estimate for tree $i$.

\subsubsection{Summary per area}

The \texttt{summary\_per\_area} function propagates area uncertainty:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_{\text{per\_area}} = |\text{value\_per\_area}| \sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_{\text{total}}}{\text{total}}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\sigma_{\text{area}}}{\text{area}}\right)^2}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Coefficient of Variation}

The per-tree coefficient of variation across methods as
\begin{equation}
  \text{CV}_i = \frac{\sigma_i}{\mu_i}, \quad \mu_i = \frac{1}{4}\sum_k x_{ik}
\end{equation}
where $\mu_i$ is the mean carbon estimate across the allometric methods for tree $i$, and $\sigma_i$ is the standard deviation of those four estimates.

where $\mu_i$ is the mean carbon estimate across the four methods (WCC, BIOMASS, allodb, Bunce) for tree $i$, and $\sigma_i$ is the standard deviation of those four estimates.


\subsection{Study site}

The analyses presented here use tree inventory data from Wakehurst Place (Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew), West Sussex, UK. Wakehurst comprises mixed temperate woodland with three habitat types: broadleaf (0.79\,ha sampled), conifer (0.44\,ha), and coppice (5.17\,ha), with dominant species including \textit{Corylus avellana}, \textit{Picea abies}, \textit{Fagus sylvatica}, and \textit{Fraxinus excelsior}. The site is managed for conservation and research. The results illustrate methodological differences but may vary with site conditions, species composition, and DBH distribution. Consequently, extrapolation to other UK woodlands should be treated with caution.

Wakehurst tree data, analysis scripts, and results are stored within the package in the \texttt{inst/paper\_scripts} directory.

\subsection{Shiny applications}

The TreeCarbon package ships two interactive Shiny applications so that users \emph{without R programming experience} can apply the same estimators as the command-line package: once R and TreeCarbon (with suggested dependencies) are installed, no script authoring or package development is needed---only pasting two short launch commands into an R console. The apps implement the \emph{same} WCC, BIOMASS, allodb, and Bunce logic described above and used in the Wakehurst analyses; they add no new allometric models but make method comparison, exploration, and reporting accessible from a browser-style interface and thereby support transparency for practitioners and reviewers who do not write code.

\textbf{(1) WCC Carbon Calculator.} Users enter or upload inventory information---typically species (or common name), DBH (cm), and height (m), or a CSV of trees---and choose WCC-relevant options where offered. Outputs include per-tree and summary above-ground carbon, tables, and interactive plots, with options to download results for use in spreadsheets or reports.

\textbf{(2) Allometry methods comparison.} Users supply species identifiers, DBH, and height (e.g.\ via CSV with columns including genus, species, DBH in centimetres, and height in metres) and run WCC, BIOMASS, allodb, and Bunce side by side. The interface supports comparative visuals, sensitivity-style exploration, and export (including spreadsheet export when optional packages are installed).

\textbf{Launch.} After \texttt{library(TreeCarbon)}, each app is started with one command at the R prompt:\nopagebreak
\begin{verbatim}
shiny::runApp(system.file("shiny-app1", package = "TreeCarbon"))
shiny::runApp(system.file("shiny-app2", package = "TreeCarbon"))
\end{verbatim}
\vspace{-0.75\baselineskip}
\noindent The first launches the WCC calculator; the second launches the multi-method comparison app. Full installation steps and suggested packages are documented in the repository README; \texttt{plotly}, \texttt{DT}, \texttt{shinyBS}, and \texttt{writexl} (spreadsheet export) are optional but recommended for full functionality.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Method choice introduces substantial uncertainty in carbon estimates}

Analysis of 2,173 trees across three UK woodland habitats at Wakehurst Place (broadleaf, conifer, coppice; total area 6.4\,ha) revealed that choice of allometric method introduces substantial uncertainty in carbon estimates. WCC requires height for all trees; BIOMASS, allodb, and Bunce require height for their non-sapling equations, so 823 trees with missing height were excluded from the four-method comparison, yielding $n = 2{,}173$ trees with estimates from all four methods.

The median CV was 32.5\% (bootstrap 95\% CI: 31.2--33.8\%, $n = 500$), with mean 54.3\% and maximum 200\%. Thirty-seven per cent of trees (795) fell in a ``danger zone'' (CV $>$ 40\%), where method choice materially affects the carbon estimate and careful justification is required. Danger-zone trees had lower mean DBH (19\,cm) and height (5.8\,m) than the overall sample (26\,cm, 11\,m), indicating that method choice is most consequential for smaller trees. Coppice showed the highest mean method CV (62\%), followed by broadleaf (45\%) and conifer (36\%).

Total carbon estimates varied by nearly threefold across methods. For the full Wakehurst inventory ($n = 2{,}996$ trees with WCC; $n = 2{,}173$ with all four methods), WCC yielded 1,672\,t\,C, whereas BIOMASS, allodb, and Bunce yielded 562, 778, and 677\,t\,C respectively. The range ratio $R = \max_k C_k / \min_k C_k$ was 2.98 (bootstrap 95\% CI: 2.95--3.02), where $C_k$ is total carbon from method $k$. Carbon per hectare varied by habitat and method: WCC gave 448\,t\,C/ha (broadleaf), 238\,t\,C/ha (coppice), and 182\,t\,C/ha (conifer), whereas BIOMASS consistently produced lower estimates (141--179\,t\,C/ha).

\subsection{Parameter sensitivity and measurement uncertainty}

Sensitivity analysis showed that carbon estimates are most sensitive to DBH (elasticity 1.98), followed by height (0.68); wood density had negligible direct effect at the default settings. A 10\% error in DBH propagated to approximately 20\% error in carbon, whereas a 10\% height error produced approximately 7\% carbon error. Under typical measurement scenarios (5\% DBH, 10\% height error), total measurement uncertainty was $\sim$9\%, but method choice uncertainty (median CV 32.5\%) exceeded measurement uncertainty. Method disagreement was strongest for small trees: mean CV was 76\% for trees with DBH 0--20\,cm, compared with 21--34\% for trees above 20\,cm.

\subsection{Protocol compliance: arithmetic vs.\ quadratic mean DBH}

Comparison of two implementations of the FC Woodland Carbon Code stand-level method (Method D; stand-level using top height and quadratic mean DBH)---one using arithmetic mean DBH and one using the protocol-specified quadratic mean DBH (QMD)---showed that protocol compliance substantially affects stand-level carbon estimates. Stand-level totals increased by 75.7\% (307 to 540\,t\,C) when switching from arithmetic mean to QMD. By habitat, broadleaf and coppice increased by $\sim$90\%, and conifer by 14.5\%. DBH was right-skewed (skewness $= 1.49$), so QMD exceeded the arithmetic mean in all habitats. Individual-tree methods (WCC, BIOMASS, allodb, Bunce) were largely unaffected by protocol rounding rules (tariff rounding, DBH floor).

\subsection{Large-tree dominance}

Cumulative carbon analysis (trees ranked by DBH, largest first) showed that the largest 10\% of trees (217 trees) held 27.6\% of total carbon, and the largest 20\% (434 trees) held 34.7\%. Approximately 88\% of trees were required to account for 50\% of total carbon. This pattern supports \citet{Calders2022}: a small fraction of large trees holds a disproportionate share of carbon, and calibration bias in this size class can materially affect stand-level estimates.

\subsection{WCC inventory and aggregation methods}

For the same Wakehurst inventory, WCC-compliant methods yielded totals ranging from 288\,t\,C to 1,672\,t\,C (Table~\ref{tab:wcc-methods}), illustrating that choice of inventory design and aggregation method can affect reported carbon stocks as much as choice of allometric equation. Stand-level Method D (\texttt{fc\_stand\_carbon}; using top height and quadratic mean DBH) yielded 540\,t\,C across the three habitats. Method B/C (sample-level tariff by habitat, with floor of mean tariff) yielded 288\,t\,C. A DBH-class tally approach (Method E style) produced 609\,t\,C. Individual-tree WCC (sum of tree-level estimates) yielded 1,672\,t\,C. The large spread reflects differences in inventory design (full census vs.\ sample), aggregation (individual-tree sum vs.\ stand mean), and DBH treatment (quadratic mean for Method D reduces effective tree size in right-skewed distributions).

\section{Discussion}

The analysis reveals that the choice of method introduces substantial uncertainty in carbon estimates. This uncertainty often exceeds the measurement error, highlighting that methodological transparency is as important as measurement precision. The strong sensitivity to DBH reinforces the importance of accurate diameter measurement, particularly where large trees hold a disproportionate share of stand carbon.

The high method disagreement for large trees (DBH $>$ 60\,cm) aligns with \citet{Calders2022}, who found that large trees are under-represented in allometric calibrations. Our cumulative carbon analysis (top 10\% of trees holding 28\% of carbon) provides a UK temperate example of this pattern. The Woodland Carbon Code equations are highly specific to UK forestry conditions and species. For trees outside the calibrated range, WCC may produce unrealistic estimates. TreeCarbon addresses this by enabling comparison with alternative methods (allodb, BIOMASS) that may be more appropriate for non-standard cases. The BIOMASS package implements pan-tropical allometric equations; when applied to UK temperate forests, BIOMASS typically produces smaller carbon estimates compared to UK-specific methods (WCC, Bunce) or temperate-focused methods (allodb). This underestimation arises from fundamental differences between tropical and temperate tree architecture.

Terrestrial laser scanning (TLS) provides direct, non-destructive measurement of tree volume. Studies comparing allometric estimates with TLS-derived biomass consistently show that allometric equations systematically underestimate carbon stocks compared to TLS measurements \citep{Calders2022, Demol2022}. This underestimation is particularly pronounced for large trees. TreeCarbon's integration of multiple methods, while valuable for understanding methodological uncertainty, cannot overcome the fundamental limitations of allometric approaches when applied to trees outside their calibration ranges. Future improvements to carbon accounting will require expanded calibration datasets that better represent the full range of tree sizes, species, and growth forms found in UK woodlands.

\section{Conclusion}

TreeCarbon provides a unified, open-source framework for comparing tree carbon estimation methods in the UK with error propagation methods. Our analysis of 2,173 trees at Wakehurst showed that method choice often introduces greater uncertainty than measurement error, and that both protocol compliance (e.g.\ quadratic mean DBH vs.\ arithmetic mean) and choice of WCC inventory method (individual-tree vs.\ stand-level vs.\ tally) can substantially affect reported carbon stocks. We recommend that method choice and uncertainty are reported clearly with estimates as the assumptions behind each method directly affects the estimate.
